DimmerKV: dim the KV cache instead of deleting it

A language model's memory of what it has read (the KV cache) is large. The usual way to shrink it is to throw tokens away, which loses facts that a later question needs. DimmerKV keeps every token, at a precision that matches how much it matters, and sharpens the right tokens when a question arrives.

The problem

For every token a model has read, it stores a key and a value vector in every layer and attention head. For Llama-3.2-3B that is about 112 KB per token, so a 16,000-token conversation needs 1.75 GB. A server that keeps conversations between turns runs out of memory quickly, and recomputing a conversation from its text costs a full re-read.

The common fix is eviction (H2O, SnapKV): keep the tokens that received the most attention and delete the rest. That works when the model already knows what it will be asked. When the question comes after compression, the facts it needs were never heavily attended, so they are the first to go.

Measured: before any question exists, the hidden facts rank at the 30–35th percentile of H2O's attention scores. H2O then answers 0 of 36 such questions on the 3B model, at every budget we tried, while the full cache answers 36 of 36.

Our idea: don't make a keep-or-delete decision. Keep every token, but at a precision ("brightness") chosen per token, so a byte budget is spent where it matters, and refine the relevant tokens once the question is known.

What we tried, in order

Every step was tested on the same "needle" test: facts like "The access code for the Orion locker is 7311" hidden in WikiText, then asked about. Budgets are the compressed size as a share of the normal FP16 cache (2% = 50× smaller).

  1. Per-head PCA codecFit a PCA basis per layer and head; store each vector as its top components. Too lossy: 1/18 facts at 27% memory. dropped
  2. Lexico sparse dictionaryEach vector picks a few of 4,096 learned atoms. Far more accurate per byte (16/18 at 27%), but encoding was slow. kept as ablation
  3. DimmerKV allocator with H2O scoresPrecision per token by attention × error. Failed after compression (0/18): attention points away from the facts. This is the finding that shaped everything after. dropped as the default signal
  4. "Flat" allocation by error per byteGive bytes to tokens that are hard to compress, ignoring attention. Doubled uniform Lexico at equal bytes (10 vs 5 of 18 at 15%). kept
  5. Question-window scoresWhen the question is in the prompt, score tokens by the question's attention (SnapKV-style) and grade precision instead of evicting: 17/18 at 15% vs SnapKV 10/18. kept
  6. kvtc-style transform codingConcatenate all layers and heads per token, remove RoPE from keys, one cross-layer PCA, grouped 0/2/4/8-bit quantization (Staniszewski & Łańcucki, ICLR 2026). About 25× smaller with near-full accuracy and 10× faster than Lexico. became the codec
  7. DimmerKV on kvtcPer-token precision on top of kvtc beat kvtc at equal bytes in every needle setting; the biggest gain with question scores (ans p .76 → .91 at 3%). kept
  8. Real storage and serving benchmarksBit-packed bytes plus DEFLATE, restored exactly; measured in MB, ms and tokens/s on 3K–16K-token conversations. kept
  9. Progressive bit-planes + question-time refinementEncode once at 8 bits, store planes most-important first; keep a small hot prefix, fetch the rest only for the tokens the question needs. Fixed most of the "question after compression" gap: ans p .50 → .71 (full .75) for 0.5% extra bytes. kept
  10. Eviction as a zero-byte precision levelLets DimmerKV mix dropping with graded precision under one budget. With question scores it matched the full cache at 1.5–2% (21–22 vs 21 of 24); SnapKV got 6–10. kept
  11. Partial-basis decodeDecode only the PCA groups that carry bits. Restore 4× faster; on the 3B model restoring became 2.3× faster than re-reading. kept
  12. Attention over the compressed cacheKeys rebuilt one layer at a time, values never decoded (attention weights the coefficients, then applies the basis). Same answers on 60/60 questions; GPU memory while answering 253 MB → 18–31 MB at 2% for an 8K-token conversation, at about 0.9× the speed. kept
  13. 3B model on an AWS L40SThe same conclusions on a bigger model; the kvtc family stays at or near the full cache down to 2%, eviction collapses. validated

The pipeline we kept, and why

kvtcCross-layer transform

Per token, keys (RoPE removed) from every layer and head form one vector (8,192 numbers on 1B, 28,672 on 3B), likewise values; one PCA basis fitted once on about 100K WikiText tokens.

Why: layers and heads are highly redundant, which per-head PCA and Lexico cannot exploit. It is the single biggest jump in quality: from losing facts at 10–20% to keeping them at 2–4%.

oursProgressive bit-planes

Each group of 64 components is quantized once at 8 bits; the top m bits are a valid m-bit code. Pieces (group, plane) are ordered once by error removed per byte.

Why: one encoding serves every precision, so tiers, refinement and demotion need no re-encoding. It is also 10–30% more accurate than kvtc's fixed 0/2/4/8-bit ladder at the same size.

oursDimmerKV allocator

Measures every token's error at every precision step and hands out upgrades greedily by error removed per byte, under one byte budget, with a zero-byte "dropped" level.

Why: attention before the question misleads, so the default weight is flat (error only); when the question is visible, its attention weights the errors. Graded precision beats keep-or-drop at equal bytes.

oursQuestion-time refinement

When a question arrives, score the restored hot cache with it, fetch the remaining planes for the top ~1% of tokens, and rebuild only those.

Why: it uses the real question instead of guessing importance in advance, which is the only signal that worked when the question comes later.

oursStorage and fast restore

Bit-packed codes, FP16 scales, per-token level ids, then DEFLATE. Restore decodes only PCA groups that carry bits and re-applies RoPE.

Why: the byte accounting is real (stored = counted + 0.1%) and restore must beat re-reading, or compression costs latency.

StreamingLLM / kvtcExact sinks and recent window

The first 4 tokens and the last 32 stay exact FP16.

Why: models attend to these heavily; compressing them hurts disproportionately for a tiny saving.

What we dropped

  • Per-head PCA: loses facts below ~40% of FP16.
  • Lexico as the main codec: 2–5× less accurate than kvtc at equal bytes and 5–10× slower to encode; kept as an ablation (it is a required track paper).
  • H2O's summed attention as importance: it ranks the facts below average before the question exists; kept as the baseline and as the scoring idea behind question-window scores.
  • Mixing codecs for keys and values: keys are the fragile stream; mixes were always worse.

Architecture

How the pieces connect: calibration runs once per model, compression once per conversation, restore and refinement once per question. One method-name registry (methods.py) feeds every evaluation script and the demo.

Limits, stated plainly

  • Answering directly from the compressed cache gives identical answers with 8–14× less GPU memory at 2%, but runs at about 0.9× the speed at 2% and 0.6–0.7× at 5%. It covers the kvtc family; the progressive codec still rebuilds an FP16 cache, and prefill still builds the full cache once.
  • Without the question, per-token precision alone is only slightly better than kvtc (often tied at 8K tokens); the gains come from question scores and question-time refinement.
  • The 3B model is near the ceiling on our needle test at 2%, so methods are hard to separate there; harder tasks and an 8B run are next.
  • Our kvtc is simplified: greedy bit allocation instead of the paper's dynamic program, fixed groups of 64, zlib on the CPU. Numbers are ours, not a reproduction.
  • Per-token precision can lose to uniform kvtc when every token matters equally: on key-value retrieval of random UUIDs kvtc scores 0.65–0.70 at 5% vs 0.35–0.55 for DimmerKV. A floor at kvtc’s precision plus question scores ties kvtc (0.65); the flat variant does not.
  • Small samples: 18–36 questions per setting; differences of 1–2 answers are noise.
